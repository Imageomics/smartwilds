\section*{Abstract}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Wildlife monitoring programmes increasingly deploy multiple sensor modalities---camera traps, autonomous recording units (ARUs), drones, citizen science observations, and unstructured text data (field notes, metadata, observation logs)---yet no standardised framework exists for evaluating how these modalities complement one another in capturing biodiversity, or for guiding sensor selection given a particular research question and budget. We present a multi-sensor fusion methodology that organises ecological monitoring around four fundamental questions---\textit{Who} (species or individual identity), \textit{Where} (spatial distribution), \textit{When} (temporal patterns), and \textit{What} (behaviour, presence, habitat use, inter-individual interactions)---each at varying resolution, and maps six co-deployed sensing modalities onto this framework.

    \item We deployed all six modalities simultaneously at The Wilds, a 10,000-acre conservation park in southeastern Ohio, USA, across multiple field campaigns. For each modality we measured species richness, relative abundance, and land-use detection using a unified observation schema (species, location, time, sensor). We quantified per-modality detection performance using AI-assisted annotation pipelines (MegaDetector, BirdNET, YOLO, BioCLIP) and LLM-based text extraction, validated against expert ground truth. We computed complementarity via species accumulation curves, cross-modal overlap matrices, and joint species distribution models (JSDMs). We evaluated both deployment costs (equipment, labour, power) and analysis costs (compute, AI inference, human review) per modality.

\section{Introduction}

% WHY: The problem
Accurate biodiversity assessment is fundamental to conservation planning, yet individual sensing modalities capture only a partial view of the species and ecological processes present at a site. Camera traps excel at detecting large mammals but miss arboreal, volant, and cryptic taxa; autonomous recording units (ARUs) detect vocalising species across broad areas but cannot identify non-vocal animals; drones provide high-resolution spatial snapshots but are temporally limited by flight duration and weather; citizen science observations offer taxonomic breadth but are spatially and temporally opportunistic; and unstructured text data---field notes, deployment metadata, and observer logs---provide rich contextual information that is rarely integrated into quantitative analyses.

% WHAT: The gap
Despite the growing availability of these technologies, most biodiversity studies rely on one or two modalities, and cross-modal comparisons are hampered by differences in data format, spatial footprint, temporal resolution, and detection probability. There is no standardised framework for (a) quantifying how well each modality captures key biodiversity metrics (species richness, abundance, land use), (b) measuring the complementarity and redundancy across modalities at a shared site, or (c) recommending cost-effective sensor combinations tailored to specific monitoring goals. More fundamentally, ecologists planning a monitoring programme face a practical question: \textit{given my budget and my research question, which sensors should I buy, which AI tools should I use, and what trade-offs in accuracy should I expect?}

% FRAMING: Who, Where, When, What
We argue that answering this question requires organising monitoring capabilities around the four fundamental dimensions of behavioural ecology research:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Who:} Identity at varying resolution---species-level classification, multi-species community composition, or individual identification.
    \item \textbf{Where:} Spatial distribution at varying resolution---site-level presence, habitat-level occupancy, or fine-grained localisation (sub-metre).
    \item \textbf{When:} Temporal patterns at varying resolution---seasonal trends, diel activity patterns, or instantaneous events.
    \item \textbf{What:} Ecological process---presence/absence, behaviour, habitat interactions, or inter-individual interactions.
\end{itemize}
Different modalities excel along different dimensions: camera traps provide strong \textit{who} and \textit{when} for large mammals but coarse \textit{where}; drones provide precise \textit{where} and \textit{what} (behaviour, posture) but sparse \textit{when}; ARUs provide continuous \textit{when} for vocal taxa but limited \textit{who} resolution below species level; citizen science provides broad \textit{who} but uncontrolled \textit{where} and \textit{when}; and text data provides contextual \textit{what} (habitat conditions, observer notes on behaviour) that enriches the interpretation of sensor data.

% HOW: What we do
We address this gap by deploying six modalities simultaneously at The Wilds, a 10,000-acre conservation park hosting diverse large-mammal, avian, and invertebrate communities. Using AI-assisted annotation pipelines and LLM-based text extraction validated against expert ground truth, we convert heterogeneous sensor outputs into a unified observation format and evaluate each modality along the Who/Where/When/What dimensions. We quantify both deployment costs (equipment, labour, power, logistics) and analysis costs (compute time, AI inference, human review hours) to provide end-to-end cost-effectiveness guidance.

% CONTRIBUTIONS
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A Who/Where/When/What framework for organising and comparing sensing modalities by their resolution along each ecological dimension, paired with a sensor$\times$research-question matrix that maps modalities to the ecological tasks they can address.
    \item A standardised multi-modal observation schema and AI-assisted annotation pipeline (including LLM-based text extraction) that enables direct cross-modal comparison of biodiversity metrics across six modalities.
    \item An empirical evaluation of six co-deployed sensing modalities for species richness, abundance estimation, and land-use detection at a single site.
    \item A complementarity analysis using species accumulation curves, cross-modal overlap, and joint species distribution models (JSDMs) that quantifies the marginal value of each additional modality.
    \item A cost model encompassing both deployment and analysis costs, with Pareto-optimal sensor combinations for different budgets and monitoring objectives.
\end{enumerate}

% SCOPE: Broad applicability per MEE requirement
Although our study site hosts managed populations of exotic ungulates and native wildlife, the methodology---unified observation schema, AI-assisted annotation, and cross-modal complementarity metrics---is broadly applicable across taxa and ecosystems wherever multiple sensing modalities can be co-deployed.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Study site}

The Wilds is a 10,000-acre (4,047 ha) conservation park in Muskingum County, Ohio, USA (39.8°N, 81.7°W). The park supports managed herds of Pere David's deer \textit{Elaphurus davidianus}, African wild dogs \textit{Lycaon pictus}, and other exotic ungulates, alongside native white-tailed deer \textit{Odocoileus virginianus}, coyote \textit{Canis latrans}, and a diverse avian community. Habitats include reclaimed grassland, mixed deciduous forest, wetlands, and lake margins. % TODO: add area of each habitat type if available

\subsection{Sensor deployment}

We conducted three field campaigns: a pilot deployment (Campaign~1, June~30--July~3, 2025), a long-term deployment (Campaign~2, August~4--28, 2025), and a supplementary drone campaign (Campaign~3, September~8, 2025). Six sensing modalities were deployed across overlapping spatial footprints:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Camera traps:} Four camera models (GardePro T5NG, GardePro A3, Trail Camera G600, Reveal Pro 3.0) at 13 site-camera combinations across Campaigns~1 and~2, with varying configurations: photo-only (4MP, 3-burst) to photo+video (4K photo, FHD video), PIR sensitivity levels from standard to level~9, and horizontal fields of view from 55\textdegree{} (Reveal Pro) to 120\textdegree{} (G600 wide-angle). Approximately 236,000 images total. Sensor configurations directly impact data arrival rates, blank image proportions, and edge compute requirements (see Section~\ref{sec:sensor_matrix}).
    \item \textbf{Autonomous recording units (ARUs):} Six Wildlife Acoustics Song Meter Micro~2 units at co-located sites, with recording schedules ranging from 5~min/hr (8.3\% duty cycle) to continuous sunrise-to-sunset ($\sim$50--60\% duty cycle). 2,453 WAV files totalling 202~GB across both campaigns.
    \item \textbf{Drones:} Parrot Anafi 4K (Campaigns~1, 2) and DJI Air~2 (Campaigns~2, 3). 16 flight sessions capturing 4K video over target pastures, lake corridors, and ungulate shelters.
    \item \textbf{Citizen science:} 991 photographs and videos from visiting researchers and students during field campaigns, providing opportunistic detections with \textit{manually annotated} species labels.
    \item \textbf{Text data:} $\sim$72 files including deployment metadata (25 files), field notes and observer logs (5~files), drone mission notes (10~files), JotForm deployment logs (12~CSV files), bioacoustic recorder summaries (8~files), and data reports (4~files). These unstructured and semi-structured text sources capture deployment context, habitat descriptions, species observations, and equipment configurations that are not encoded in sensor data streams.
\end{itemize}

Camera traps and ARUs were co-located at four site pairs (TW02, TW06, TW07, TW09) to enable direct cross-modal comparison. Drone flight paths overlapped with ground-sensor coverage areas. % TODO: include site map figure

\subsection{Unified observation schema}

To enable cross-modal comparison, we converted all modality outputs into a common tabular format with fields: \texttt{datetime} (ISO 8601), \texttt{latitude}, \texttt{longitude}, \texttt{site\_id}, \texttt{modality}, \texttt{species}, \texttt{count}, and \texttt{confidence}. Text data records were parsed using LLM-based extraction to identify species mentions, habitat descriptions, and temporal references, and linked to sensor observations by site ID and date. The schema is extensible to additional observation details (sex, age, behaviour) as annotation matures.

\subsection{Biodiversity metrics}

For each modality and for multi-modal fusion, we computed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Species richness:} The number of distinct species detected. We constructed species accumulation curves (rarefaction) per modality and for cumulative multi-modal combinations to quantify sampling completeness and marginal discovery rate.
    \item \textbf{Relative abundance:} The count of individuals per species, standardised by sampling effort (trap-nights for camera traps, recorder-hours for ARUs, flight-hours for drones, collar-days for GPS, observer-hours for citizen science).
    \item \textbf{Land-use detection:} Presence/absence of each species at each site, aggregated over the deployment period, to assess spatial coverage of habitat use.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-modal complementarity analysis}

\subsubsection{Species overlap matrix}

We constructed a modality-by-modality overlap matrix measuring the Jaccard similarity $J(A, B) = |S_A \cap S_B| / |S_A \cup S_B|$ of detected species sets $S_A$ and $S_B$ for each pair of modalities. Low Jaccard values indicate high complementarity.

\subsubsection{Species-by-modality heatmap}

A binary detection matrix (species $\times$ modality) was visualised as a heatmap to identify which taxa are uniquely or redundantly detected across modalities.

\subsubsection{Joint species distribution model}

To estimate habitat usage patterns that emerge only from cross-modal integration, we fitted a joint species distribution model (JSDM) following the Hierarchical Modelling of Species Communities (HMSC) framework \citep{% TODO: cite Ovaskainen & Abrego 2020 %}. The JSDM simultaneously models species occurrence as a function of environmental covariates and residual species-to-species associations. We compared model performance (AUC, Tjur's $R^2$) when fitted on: (a) each modality alone, (b) pairwise combinations, and (c) all modalities fused.

We defined three composite metrics for cross-modal comparison:
\begin{align}
\text{Coverage Efficiency} &= \frac{\text{Spatial Range} \times \text{Temporal Range}}{\text{Operator Effort}} \label{eq:coverage} \\
\text{Data Resolution Index} &= f(\text{Spatial Resolution}, \text{Temporal Resolution}) \label{eq:resolution} \\
\text{Species Coverage Score} &= \frac{\text{Number of species detectable}}{\text{Effort required}} \label{eq:species_score}
\end{align}

These composite metrics were visualised as radar charts to provide an intuitive comparison across modalities.

\subsection{Cost-effectiveness analysis}

We decomposed total cost per modality into two components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deployment costs:} Equipment purchase/rental, batteries/power, SD cards/storage media, field labour (installation, check-ins, retrieval), travel, and logistics.
    \item \textbf{Analysis costs:} Compute time (GPU-hours for AI inference), cloud/HPC costs, human review time (expert validation, manual annotation), and software licensing.
\end{itemize}

% TODO: Fill in actual cost figures per modality
We normalised total cost per detected species, per unit spatial-temporal coverage, and per research question addressed (from the sensor matrix, Table~\ref{table:sensor_matrix}). This enables programme designers to answer: \textit{given a budget of \$X and a need to answer research question Y, which sensor combination is Pareto-optimal?} We identified Pareto-optimal modality combinations at multiple budget levels and monitoring objectives.

\subsection{Ablation study}

To quantify the marginal contribution of each modality, we performed an ablation study: we computed species richness, abundance estimates, and JSDM model performance when systematically removing one modality at a time from the full six-modality fusion, and when adding modalities incrementally in order of cost.
